## Where Do Landing Pages Stand Going Into 2027?

The page matters as much as it ever did, and you control less of what happens around it. The ad platforms choose more of your traffic, sometimes choose the page itself, and charge more for the click. What's left in your hands is whether the page keeps the promise that brought the visitor there.

I've been writing response pages since they were reply cards in a direct-mail pack. That's 40 years, with $523M+ in tracked results across the record. This report sets the published data beside what I've seen in my own work this year, including two tests of my own that didn't go the way I wanted. Where a number is mine and not a published benchmark, I'll say so.

If you want the craft itself, it's in my guide to [landing page copywriting](/blog/landing-page-copywriting). This piece is about what changed.

## What Changed Since the 2026 Edition?

I published the first edition in February 2026. Here's what moved, and what I'd take back.

**Google started choosing the page.** In its AI Max for Search campaigns, Google's [help page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16230205?hl=en) says "Final URL expansion is enabled by default" and that "Google may replace your Final URL with a more relevant landing page based on the user's search query." Performance Max works the same way.

**AI page builders arrived, with thin evidence.** You can now generate a complete landing page from a prompt. The claims for how those pages convert come almost entirely from the companies selling them.

**Accessibility became enforceable.** The European Accessibility Act began to apply in June 2025, and US lawsuits kept climbing.

**Third-party cookies stayed.** Google [confirmed](https://privacysandbox.google.com/blog/privacy-sandbox-next-steps) in April 2025 that it had "made the decision to maintain our current approach to offering users third-party cookie choice in Chrome." The cookieless future that a lot of personalization advice was built around didn't arrive on schedule.

**Mobile's share of buying kept rising.** Adobe reported that in the 2025 holiday season smartphones took [the majority of US online transactions](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/01/adobe-holiday-shopping-season), 56.4 percent.

### What I got wrong, and right, last year

I said the landing page had split into two classes, dynamic personalized pages and static ones, and that personalization was the main thing separating them. I overstated that. Personalization helps when the page underneath already sells. Most of the gap I see between pages that convert and pages that don't is still message match, the first screen and the offer.

I quoted conversion lifts of 30 to 60 percent from moving a static page to a personalized one. Those are my working numbers from projects I've been involved in, and I haven't found independent data that supports a figure that high. Treat them as the top of what's possible, not what to expect.

I said privacy rules and the end of third-party cookies would push everyone to first-party data. First-party data is still the right bet. The cookie deadline I leaned on was dropped.

I said mobile-first design was no longer optional. Every number published since has agreed.

## What Is a Good Landing Page Conversion Rate?

The most useful public benchmark is a median of 6.6 percent, and you should read the small print before comparing yourself to it.

Unbounce's [Conversion Benchmark Report](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/) analyzed more than 41,000 landing pages, 464 million visitors and 57 million conversions between July 2023 and July 2024, and found that "the median conversion rate across all industries is 6.6%." By sector, its [e-commerce median](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/ecommerce-conversion-rate/) is 4.2 percent and its [SaaS median](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/saas-conversion-rate/) is 3.8 percent. It's the latest edition I can find, so the data is a little over a year old.

A "conversion" in that data is whatever the page was built to collect: a sale, a lead, a free trial, a webinar sign-up. A free sign-up page and a page asking for a credit card don't belong on the same scale.

For pages that sell or generate qualified leads from paid traffic, these are my own working ranges:

- **Average pages: 2 to 5 percent.** A template, generic copy, little testing.
- **Well-built pages: 8 to 15 percent.** Strong copy, real message match, steady testing.
- **The best pages on warm traffic: 15 to 30 percent.** An audience that already knows you, and an offer that fits.

From my own work: the cold-traffic funnel I wrote for Conor Reynolds converts at 8 percent at the sales page, and the Abundant text sales letter reached 340 percent ROAS on Facebook cold traffic. Both were long pages with one job.

Downstream of the landing page, the leak is bigger still. The Baymard Institute's [running average of 50 studies](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate) puts cart abandonment at 70.22 percent. In its survey of why, the most common reason was extra costs such as shipping, tax and fees being too high. A landing page that's vague about the total price is only postponing that moment.

## What Kind of Copy Converts?

Plain copy, at a length that fits the decision.

The clearest finding in the Unbounce data is about reading level: "Landing pages written at a 5th to 7th grade level convert at 11.1%," which the report says is more than twice the rate of professional-level writing, at 5.3 percent. The pattern holds inside sectors too.

That matches everything I've seen in 40 years. Short words and short sentences aren't a style preference. A visitor from a paid ad gives you a few seconds of half-attention, usually on a phone, and anything that takes effort to read loses them.

On length, Unbounce points to 285 to 930 words for an e-commerce page that hits the median and 250 to 725 for SaaS. Don't read that as a rule that short wins. A page that captures an email address can be short. A page that asks a stranger for $200 has more objections to answer, and it earns the right to more words by answering them. I've set out how I decide in [how long a sales page should be](/blog/how-long-should-a-sales-page-be).

Three rules I work to on every page:

**The guarantee comes before the price.** The reader should meet the price with the risk already removed.

**With two options, the higher-priced one goes first.** It sets the anchor, so the second reads as the sensible choice.

**When the ad goes straight to a product page, the product page is a cold page.** It has to retell the scene from the ad and explain the mechanism. A buy box and five bullet points isn't enough for someone who met you ten seconds ago.

## Why Does Mobile Still Convert Worse?

Because most pages are still designed on a desktop and checked on a phone afterward.

Statcounter put mobile at [59.9 percent of worldwide web traffic](https://gs.statcounter.com/platform-market-share/desktop-mobile-tablet) in October 2026. Contentsquare's [2026 Digital Experience Benchmark](https://contentsquare.com/guides/digital-experience-benchmark/conversions/), drawn from 99 billion sessions, found that "desktop conversion rate was 74% higher than mobile."

Some of that gap is behavior you can't change. People look on the phone and buy on the laptop. The rest is the page, and the place to look is the first screen.

I design the first phone screen before anything else is built: headline, subhead, image and button, at phone width. If the first screen a visitor sees is a large picture with the headline somewhere below it, the page is broken, however good it looks on a monitor. I won't run traffic to a page like that.

A smaller trap: phones hide scrollbars until a finger moves. A panel of testimonials or a pricing table that scrolls sideways looks, on a phone, like something that's been cut off. If it has to scroll, it needs a visible cue.

## What Is Page Speed Worth?

Real money, though the famous numbers are older than most people quoting them realize.

Google's [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) set the bar: the main content should load within 2.5 seconds, the page should respond to a tap within 200 milliseconds, and the layout shouldn't jump around. The [2025 Web Almanac](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/performance) found that 48 percent of mobile sites passed all three, up from 44 percent the year before. So more than half of mobile sites still fail.

The evidence that it matters:

- A Deloitte study for Google, [Milliseconds Make Millions](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions), followed 37 brand sites and more than 30 million sessions at the end of 2019. It reported that a 0.1 second improvement in mobile speed raised conversion rates by 8.4 percent for retail sites and 10.1 percent for travel sites. That data is now seven years old.
- [Rakuten 24](https://web.dev/case-studies/rakuten) ran an A/B test of a page optimized for Core Web Vitals against the original and measured a "33.13% increase in conversion rate" and a "53.37% increase in revenue per visitor."

The usual culprits on a landing page are a heavy hero image, a video that loads before anyone asks for it, and a stack of tracking scripts. None of those needs to load before the visitor can read the headline, so I keep them out of the first screen's way.

## How Much of the Landing Page Do the Ad Platforms Control Now?

More than most advertisers have noticed.

Start with the setting I mentioned above. In Google's AI campaign types, your chosen landing page is a suggestion unless you say otherwise. For Performance Max, Google's [documentation](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14337539?hl=en) says "Final URL expansion is turned on by default for Performance Max campaigns." If you've built a page to continue a specific ad, and the system sends the click to your home page or a blog post, your message match is gone and your conversion data is describing a different page than you think. You can turn it off, or exclude URLs. Either way, check it.

Then there's the old question of whether the page affects what you pay. Google's [Quality Score page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6167118?hl=en) lists landing page experience, "how relevant and useful your landing page is to people who click your ad," as one of three components. The same page says "Quality Score is not an input in the ad auction." It's a diagnostic. A poor page still costs you, in the sales it doesn't make.

On Meta, the ad system now tests enormous numbers of creative variations, which I covered in my [State of VSL Marketing](/blog/state-of-vsl-marketing-2027) report. The consequence for the page is the one advertisers handle worst. Twenty ads with twenty different promises all land on one page with one headline, and nineteen of those visitors arrive at a page that seems to be about something else.

### The message match check I run

Before any page goes live, I put the ad and the page side by side and check six things.

1. **The promise.** The page's headline should finish the sentence the ad started.
2. **The mechanism name.** If the ad names it, the page uses the identical words, not a synonym.
3. **The offer numbers.** Price, discount, guarantee length, shipping. Every number in the ad matches the page exactly.
4. **The awareness hand-off.** An ad that shows and names the product can't land on a page that pretends the product is a secret. An ad that never mentions a product can't land on a buy box.
5. **The call to action.** "Shop Now" lands on something you can buy within a screen. "Learn More" lands on something to read.
6. **The claims.** The page makes no claim the ad avoided, and keeps every qualifier the ad carried.

Rows three, four and six are the ones that lose the sale, or the ad account.

## Do AI-Built and Personalized Pages Convert Better?

Sometimes, and nobody independent has shown how often.

The result you'll see quoted is from Crazy Egg, which [tested an AI-built landing page](https://www.crazyegg.com/blog/ai-vs-human-landing-page/) against a human-built one and reported that "the AI landing page had a 44.83% conversion lift." Crazy Egg sells the tool, the conversion figures rest on a few hundred visitors, and the author says one test doesn't prove AI pages win consistently. I haven't found a neutral study.

Set that beside what large-scale testing shows. Optimizely [reviewed 173,000 experiments](https://www.optimizely.com/insights/top-10-takeaways-from-the-experimentation-playbook/) and found that "only 12% of experiments produce a statistically significant improvement on the primary metric." Most changes to a page do nothing you can measure.

I learned this the expensive way in the last few months, on my own pages.

**The lander test.** In October and November 2026 I ran paid ads to two landing pages of my own. A few days in, I rewrote the hero of one page so its headline matched the best-performing ad. Over the next three days engaged sessions went from 2 in 18 to 8 in 19. It looked like a fourfold lift. Over the full run the page settled at about 20 percent engaged. Across both pages, 96 visits produced 9 clicks on the booking calendar and no bookings, and I paused the campaign. The hero was never the problem. The page was asking a cold visitor from an ad to book a call, and that was too big a step.

**The portfolio rebuild.** In October I rebuilt my [portfolio](/portfolio) page because its main button sent visitors off to a shared folder of samples. I put six excerpts on the page itself. Over the following five weeks, clicks out to the folder fell from 78 to 13, and there were 29 clicks through to individual samples. That part worked. Visits that reached the booking page didn't move. Fixing the leak I could see didn't fix the next step.

I take two things from those. A result from a few dozen visitors is noise until it holds. And the biggest gains are in what the page asks for and what it offers, which is where I'd now start any test.

My rule for personalized pages follows from that. Write the master page as a complete sales letter that works for every visitor, with the strongest arguments visible to all. Then add dynamic pieces only where knowing something about the visitor makes the argument more convincing. A page that swaps in the visitor's city has shown it knows where they are. It hasn't given them a reason to buy. There's more in my guide to [personalization and CRO](/blog/personalization-conversion-rate-optimization).

## What Is Legally Riskier Than It Was?

Accessibility, fake urgency and hidden costs. I'm a copywriter and this isn't legal advice.

**Accessibility.** The [European Accessibility Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng) covers services provided to consumers after 28 June 2025, and e-commerce is on its list. In the US there's no single rule, but there are plaintiffs. The law firm Seyfarth [reported](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/federal-court-website-accessibility-1182174/) that "plaintiffs filed 3,117 website accessibility lawsuits in federal court in 2025," which it called "a 27% increase from 2024." Text contrast you can read, form fields with labels, a page that works from a keyboard and captions on video are the basics, and each of them also helps the visitor who's squinting at a phone in daylight.

**Fake urgency.** In October 2026 the UK's competition regulator fined Wayfair 4.8 million pounds over time-limited offers that weren't time-limited, countdown timers included. I went through the cases in the [VSL report](/blog/state-of-vsl-marketing-2027). A timer that resets when the page reloads is now a documented way to get fined.

**Hidden costs.** Extra costs at checkout are the top reason shoppers give Baymard for abandoning a cart. Showing the full price on the landing page is the honest thing to do and, on the evidence, the profitable one.

## Where I'd Start in 2027

In this order.

1. **Find out where your paid clicks land.** Check the URL expansion settings and the landing page report.
2. **Run the six-row match check** on your three highest-spending ads.
3. **Open the page on a phone.** Judge the first screen and nothing else.
4. **Question the ask.** If a cold visitor is being asked for a call, a long form or a large payment, test a smaller first step.
5. **Rewrite at a lower reading level.**
6. **Fix speed and accessibility** together. The work overlaps.
7. **Then test,** one big difference at a time, and wait for the numbers.
