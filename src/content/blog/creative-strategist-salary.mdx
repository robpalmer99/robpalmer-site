## Why the Numbers Are Climbing

Salary surveys tell you what the market pays. They do not tell you why. The why matters, because it tells you whether these numbers are a bubble or a floor.

The demand is structural. When Meta rolled out its Andromeda ranking system, ad targeting stopped being something advertisers controlled and became something the algorithm infers from the creative itself. That flipped the leverage in paid social: creative volume and diversity now determine performance more than audience selection does. A brand spending $50,000 a month needs 15 to 25 net-new concepts monthly to avoid creative fatigue. At $500,000 a month, the working number is 40 to 60. Somebody has to own the system that generates, tests, and learns from that volume — and that somebody is the creative strategist.

The hiring data reflects it. LinkedIn currently lists more than <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/jobs/creative-strategist-jobs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">11,000 open creative strategist positions</a> in the United States. Meanwhile, an <a href="https://bloomberry.com/blog/i-analyzed-180m-jobs-to-see-what-jobs-ai-is-actually-replacing-today/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">analysis of 180 million job postings</a> found writer roles — copywriters, copy editors, technical writers — declined 28% year over year in 2025, while roles involving creative direction and strategy held or grew. The market is repricing where the craft gets applied: away from producing individual assets, toward owning the testing loop. I wrote about that bifurcation at length in [Is Copywriting Dead?](/blog/is-copywriting-dead) — the short version is that the craft survived and got promoted.

One more number worth holding onto: industry attribution studies consistently find that creative quality accounts for roughly half of a paid campaign's sales impact, more than targeting, bidding, or placement combined. When the single biggest lever in a seven-figure ad budget is the creative, the $12,000 a month you pay the person running the creative testing system is the highest-ROI line item on the budget.

## What Separates the Top of the Band From the Bottom

Every band above has a 40% spread between its floor and ceiling. Three capabilities decide where an individual lands. I have watched this play out across every market I have worked in, from supplements to financial publishing to SaaS.

**Direct response craft.** The persuasion fundamentals — hooks, [awareness levels](/blog/what-is-direct-response-copywriting), benefit hierarchy, offer construction — that determine whether high-volume testing is exploring a space of strong concepts or generating fifty variations of a weak one. Strategists without this brain produce volume that all dies on the same cold-traffic math.

**AI production fluency.** The throughput difference between a strategist with a working AI workflow and one without is roughly 10x: 10 to 30 concepts shipped weekly versus 3 to 5. In a role where volume is the deliverable, that multiple shows up directly in what the operator can charge. This is the part most senior copywriters never built, and the part most junior "AI-native" strategists have nothing underneath.

**Analytical rigour.** Reading ad-account data without confusing noise for signal — sample sizes, segmentation traps, the difference between a winner that beat the control and one that got lucky on a high-spend day. Without this, the testing loop has no learning in it, and the strategist is expensive decoration.

Operators with all three are rare, which is exactly why the expert band exists and why it keeps rising. Operators with one or two — the large majority of people currently holding the title — compete on price at the bottom of each band. If you are hiring, the vetting questions that expose the difference are in the [hiring guide](/blog/hire-a-creative-strategist). If you are a copywriter considering the move, the gap analysis is your roadmap: the craft you have is the scarce part; the tooling and the data literacy are learnable.

## How Brands Should Actually Budget for This

Headcount thinking gets this wrong. The right frame is ad spend and required creative volume.

- **Under $50K/month ad spend:** a full-time hire is usually premature. A senior fractional strategist at $5,000 to $10,000 monthly gives you the brain without the salary, and your creative volume needs do not yet justify more.
- **$50K to $250K/month:** this is the zone where a dedicated strategist (in-house at $90K to $150K, or senior freelance at $8,000 to $12,000 monthly) typically pays for itself in creative win-rate improvement alone.
- **Above $250K/month:** you need a strategist plus production support, which is where the $15,000 to $25,000 full creative retainers become rational. At this spend, a 10% improvement in creative performance is worth more per month than the entire retainer.

The dangerous purchase at every tier is the cheap one. The role looks doable (anyone can generate variations) so the bottom of the market is crowded with one-brain operators charging $3,000 a month to teach your ad account the wrong lessons. A mispriced strategist wastes the retainer and burns the ad spend the retainer was supposed to optimize.
