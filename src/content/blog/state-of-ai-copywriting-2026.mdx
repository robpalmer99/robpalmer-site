## Where AI Copywriting Stands in 2026

Every January, my inbox fills with predictions that this will finally be the year AI replaces copywriters. I have been experimenting with AI writing tools for at least two years, and I have kept logs of what they produce for the last year. After four decades of [direct-response copywriting](/blog/what-is-direct-response-copywriting) and more than $523 million in tracked campaign results, my honest assessment is that the hype cycle keeps promising more than it delivers.

AI copywriting tools in 2026 are genuinely impressive. They are also insufficient for the work that moves revenue. This post covers the four things I think are specific to this year: what has changed since 2024, the AI slop problem, what it has done to pricing, and how it plays out by industry. For the fundamentals, I have written separate guides: [AI copywriting](/blog/ai-copywriting) for the overview, [AI vs human copywriting](/blog/ai-vs-human-copywriting) for the head-to-head, [AI copywriting tools](/blog/ai-copywriting-tools) for the tool comparison, and [the AI copywriting prompts I use](/blog/ai-copywriting-prompts) for the section-level drafting.

<DefinitionBox term="AI Slop">
The growing flood of mediocre, AI-generated marketing content that saturates digital channels — copy that is grammatically correct and superficially professional but strategically empty, emotionally flat, and indistinguishable from thousands of other AI-produced pieces. AI slop converts poorly because it lacks differentiation, emotional specificity, and persuasion architecture. It is a major quality problem in digital marketing in 2026.

## What Has Actually Changed Since 2024

The AI landscape has shifted in real and meaningful ways. Dismissing these improvements would be as dishonest as overhyping them.

### The models are better at following instructions

The biggest practical improvement is instruction adherence. When I give Claude or ChatGPT a detailed strategic brief in 2026 — specifying the audience, the emotional triggers, the awareness level, the persuasion framework, and the constraints — the output is meaningfully closer to what I asked for than it was in my 2024 logs. The models still require expert direction, but they waste less of my time with off-target drafts.

### Tone consistency has improved

AI tools are noticeably better at maintaining a consistent voice across long-form content. In my 2024 drafts, a 3,000-word AI output would often drift between tones — professional in one section, casual in the next, strangely formal in the conclusion. That drift has been reduced. It has not been eliminated, but the improvement is real.

### Research capabilities have expanded

The ability to synthesize large volumes of information — competitor analysis, customer review mining, market trend identification — has become the single most valuable AI capability for copywriters. I can now feed an AI tool a competitor's entire funnel and receive a structural analysis in minutes that used to take hours by hand. This is where AI delivers unambiguous, practical value.

### Specialized tools have emerged

Beyond the general-purpose models, 2026 has brought specialized tools built specifically for copywriting workflows — tools that integrate audience research, competitive analysis, and draft generation into unified platforms. Some of these are genuinely useful. Most are wrappers around the same underlying models with a marketing-friendly interface. A more effective approach is encoding proven copywriting frameworks directly into AI skill files — which is the approach behind my [free Claude Code copywriting skills](/blog/claude-code-copywriting-skills). The distinction matters when you are deciding where to invest.

## What Has Not Changed

The core limits are the same ones I wrote about in [AI vs human copywriting](/blog/ai-vs-human-copywriting): AI does not design persuasion architecture, it writes about emotion at surface level, and it still invents claims in regulated markets. ## The AI Slop Problem

If one development defines AI copywriting in 2026, it is the flood of AI slop: mediocre, machine-generated content in every marketing channel. I do not have a reliable figure for how much of it exists, and I have not found a primary source I would trust, so I will not quote one. What I can describe is what it looks like from where I sit. Most of it is barely distinguishable from one piece to the next. The same smooth-but-empty sentences. The same surface-level emotional appeals. The same generic value propositions.

### Why AI slop hurts everyone

Your prospects see more marketing messages than they used to, and the average message is blander. Cutting through now takes copy with real strategic depth and differentiation, not more copy or faster copy. The irony is sharp: the tool that was supposed to make copywriting easier has made effective copywriting harder by saturating the environment with sameness.

### How to spot AI slop in your own marketing

AI-generated copy has patterns that experienced eyes catch quickly. Paragraphs that all follow the same rhythm. Transitional filler such as "moreover," "furthermore," "in today's landscape." A habit of hedging rather than committing. Lists that default to three items. Conclusions that restate the introduction. If your copy shows these patterns, readers will register the lack of specificity even if they never think "AI wrote this," and that erodes trust.

## The Pricing Implications

AI has reshaped the economics of copywriting, with opportunity and risk on both sides.

### The collapse of commodity pricing

Basic content copywriting (blog posts, product descriptions, social captions, simple web pages) has seen dramatic price compression. Work that cost $300-$500 per piece in 2023 can now be produced for $50-$100 using AI tools with light human editing. If your needs are mostly commodity content, AI has genuinely cut your costs.

### The premium on strategic copy

At the other end, [rates for experienced direct-response copywriters](/blog/copywriting-rates) have held up. The reason is the cost of getting it wrong. I do not have a published study comparing AI-only and human-led pages, and I will not invent one. What I can point to is the work the premium pays for: the long-form text sales page I wrote for Stonehenge Health's Dynamic Brain, a 40-compound nootropic, produced $2.1M in its first 90 days, as reported by the client (see the [portfolio](/portfolio)). That page was built on research, a named condition, and a mechanism, which are the decisions I have never seen AI make well on its own. When you understand [what conversion rate optimization actually involves](/blog/what-is-conversion-rate-optimization), a small production saving is easily outweighed by a lost point of conversion on a page that carries paid traffic.

### The dangerous middle

The most exposed position in the 2026 market is the middle: copywriters who produce competent but undifferentiated work. Their output is not bad enough to be obviously worse than AI, but not good enough to justify a real premium over AI-assisted production. That tier is being squeezed from both sides.

## Industry-Specific Realities

AI copywriting does not perform uniformly across industries. The variation is significant enough to warrant separate consideration.

### Health and supplements

AI remains particularly problematic for [health-related copy](/blog/copywriting-psychology) because of the combination of regulatory sensitivity and emotional complexity. Claims that sound reasonable can violate FTC guidelines. Mechanism descriptions that seem scientific can be entirely fabricated. And the emotional specificity required to connect with prospects struggling with health issues demands the kind of human empathy that AI cannot replicate. In health copy, AI is a useful research tool and a dangerous writing tool.

### Financial services

Compliance complexity in financial copywriting makes AI-only production risky. The models understand financial concepts at a surface level but miss the nuances of what can and cannot be said in specific regulatory contexts. Human review is not optional — it is a compliance necessity.

### E-commerce and SaaS

These sectors have seen the most successful AI integration, particularly for product descriptions, feature comparisons, and email automation. The copy is more structured, less emotionally complex, and more data-driven — all areas where AI performs well. Even here, the highest-converting assets (pricing pages, trial-to-paid sequences, churning customer campaigns) benefit from human strategic direction.

### Information products and coaching

The information marketing space has been hit hardest by AI slop. The volume of AI-generated sales pages, webinar scripts, and launch sequences has created enormous noise. Paradoxically, this makes human-written copy with genuine personality, specific experience, and authentic authority more effective than ever — because it stands out in a sea of generated sameness.

## What Comes Next and What To Do

I expect the models to keep improving at instruction-following and consistency, which will further cut the cost of commodity content. I expect the gap on strategic work to narrow slowly rather than close, because persuasion strategy is a judgment about a specific market, not a text pattern. And I expect "human-led, AI-assisted" to become the standard for serious conversion work. One related shift worth watching is [GEO copywriting](/blog/generative-engine-optimization-for-copywriters), writing copy that AI search engines cite, and [how AI models evaluate copywriting expertise](/blog/how-ai-models-evaluate-copywriting-expertise).

In practical terms:

- **High-stakes conversion assets** (sales pages, VSLs, core email sequences): use an experienced copywriter who uses AI tools. The cost of underperformance dwarfs production savings.
- **Commodity content** (blog posts, product descriptions, basic web pages): AI with light human editing is a reasonable approach.
- **Testing**: use AI aggressively to generate headline, subject line and hook variations.
- **Research**: use AI without hesitation for competitor analysis and market language mining.
