The short answer: a professionally written VSL script costs between $3,000 and $50,000 or more in 2026, production adds $500 to $25,000, and senior writers often take a royalty on top. That range is wide enough to be useless on its own, so the rest of this guide shows you where your project lands in it and why.

I've written [VSLs](/services/vsl-copywriter) behind a $40 million supplement funnel, a $30 million biz-op offer that reached number one on ClickBank, and a $27 million financial promotion, and I've reviewed hundreds of other writers' scripts as Copy Chief inside Copy Accelerator. I've seen every pricing model there is. Here's what they actually cost and what moves the number.

- VSL script fees run $3,000 to $7,500 at the junior level, $7,500 to $20,000 at mid-level, and $20,000 to $50,000 or more from senior writers with documented eight-figure results

</KeyTakeaways>

<DefinitionBox term="VSL (Video Sales Letter)">
A spoken, scripted video, usually 10 to 45 minutes long, that walks a cold viewer through hook, story, mechanism, proof, offer and close without letting them skip to the price. The cost of a VSL has two parts: the script, which carries the persuasion, and the production, which turns the script into video. The script is where the conversion rate lives, which is why it carries most of the cost.
</DefinitionBox>

The junior column is what you find on freelance marketplaces. The writer has training and a few scripts, but no cold-traffic numbers to show. The mid-level column is where most working VSL writers sit: they've had wins, they can tell you a conversion rate, and they know what a hook is for. The senior column is the writers who can name the offers, the spend and how long each script held as the control.

The plus sign on the senior row matters. For a scaled offer with real revenue potential, senior writers charge above the range, often with a royalty attached, because the fee is being set against what the script will produce rather than how long it takes to write.

My advice for a new offer is to produce the test version cheaply, prove the script converts, and then spend on production. A $25,000 shoot of a script that loses the viewer at minute two is the most expensive mistake in this market, and it happens every week. I produce test-grade versions of my own scripts through an AI studio stack for exactly this reason, so a client can see the video working before they book talent.

## Royalties: how the maths works

Many senior VSL copywriters, including me on the right offer, will take a lower base fee in exchange for a share of what the script produces. A typical structure is a base of $10,000 to $25,000 plus 3 to 5 percent of net sales. Blind business-opportunity offers sometimes go higher.

Work the numbers on a modest winner. A VSL doing $2 million a year at a 4 percent royalty pays the writer $80,000 a year, which is more than the biggest flat fee on the table. That's why the best writers want royalties: it's a bet on their own work, and a client who sees a writer willing to make that bet is seeing the strongest signal of confidence available.

For the client, a royalty lowers the upfront risk and aligns the writer with the back end. The writer will want to see your traffic numbers and offer economics before agreeing to one, because a royalty on an offer with no traffic is worth nothing.

## The five things that move the price

When two writers quote $12,000 and $35,000 for the same VSL, the gap is explained by five variables. Knowing them lets you scope the project before you ask for a number.

**1. Length and price point.** A 10-minute script for a $47 impulse offer is a different job from a 40-minute script for a $997 program. Longer scripts need more proof, more objection handling and more pattern interrupts, and the writing time scales with them. Price point sets the length: higher-ticket offers need more story and more proof before the viewer reaches for a card.

**2. Research scope.** This is the biggest hidden variable. A senior writer spends the first week or more mining forums, reviews, support transcripts and competitor VSLs for the buyer's own language, and the hook comes out of that layer. A cheap script skips it and works from your product sheet. If a quote arrives before the writer has asked about your market, the research isn't in it.

**3. Hook and close variations.** A script with one hook is a single test. A script with four hooks and two closes is a testing program. Senior writers include the variations because the first split test is where the money is; mid-level writers often bill them separately. Ask what's included.

**4. Vertical and compliance load.** A supplement VSL has to make the mechanism believable without a disease claim. A financial VSL has to promise without guaranteeing. A biz-op VSL has income-claim rules on every platform. Each of those adds review time and often a second draft, and a writer who knows the vertical builds the compliance in from the first line rather than fixing it after a rejection.

**5. Revenue potential of the offer.** Experienced writers price against what the script can produce. A VSL for an offer already spending $100,000 a month on traffic justifies a different investment, and often a royalty, than a script for a product with no traffic yet. Pricing against revenue potential ties the writer's fee to the value of the work, and it's why the same writer may quote two clients very different numbers for scripts of similar length.

## What a complete VSL funnel costs

A VSL rarely runs alone. Under it sits a sales page or TSL, an order form, upsell and downsell videos, and an email follow-up sequence, and every one of those is copy.

- **VSL script:** $20,000 to $50,000+ at the senior level
- **TSL adaptation of the same script:** often included in a VSL engagement, or $5,000 to $15,000 as a separate item
- **Upsell and downsell scripts:** $5,000 to $12,000 each at the senior level
- **Email follow-up sequence:** $7,500 to $15,000+
- **Complete funnel from one senior writer:** $30,000 to $75,000+

Bundling the funnel with one writer costs less than the sum of the parts and keeps every piece on one story, which is the thing agencies with four writers on four pieces consistently get wrong. ## The ROI maths that should decide it

Here's the calculation that makes the fee irrelevant.

Say you're spending $50,000 a month on cold traffic at $2 a click, so 25,000 visitors reach the VSL. At a 0.5 percent conversion rate on a $97 offer, that's 125 sales and $12,125 in revenue. A loss. At 2 percent, it's 500 sales and $48,500. Roughly break-even on the front end, with the back end making the profit, which is how scaled offers run.

The difference between those two outcomes, on one month of spend, is $36,375. The script fee is paid once. The conversion rate compounds every month the VSL runs, and a good one holds as the control for a year or more.

That's why the senior fee is cheap and the junior fee is expensive. The $3,000 script that converts at 0.5 percent costs you the ad spend it burns, month after month, until you replace it. The $35,000 script that converts at 2 percent paid for itself before the first month ended.

## Red flags in a VSL quote

**A price before they've seen the offer.** Scope sets research, research sets results. A number before scoping is a number for a template.

**Under $3,000 from someone claiming eight-figure results.** The maths doesn't work. A writer with real results doesn't need to compete on price.

**No hooks or closes mentioned.** One hook is one test. Ask how many variations are in the fee.

**Hourly billing for a conversion asset.** Hourly punishes the efficient writer and rewards the slow one. Senior VSL writers quote the project.

**Nothing about production handoff.** If the deliverable is a prose document, your editor will make creative decisions the writer should have made, and you'll pay for the re-edits.
