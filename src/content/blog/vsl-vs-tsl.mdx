Every few weeks a supplement or info-product owner asks me the same question in the first ten minutes of a call: should we run a VSL or a TSL?

The honest answer is that the format matters less than where the traffic comes from. I've written both since the VSL format appeared, and the pattern in the numbers is consistent enough to give you a rule you can act on today. Then I'll show you the numbers behind it and the sequence I'd follow if you can only build one of the two this quarter.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A VSL controls the pace and hides the price; a TSL lets the reader skim and jump. That single difference explains most of the performance gap in either direction
- Native and YouTube cold traffic usually favours the VSL. Facebook and Instagram traffic often favours the TSL. Email is roughly even
- A TSL costs less to build, far less to test, and is easier to keep compliant. A VSL is harder to skim past and holds a cold viewer through the argument
- Most scaled offers in 2026 run a hybrid page: video on top, full text below. If you build one first, build the one that matches your main traffic source
- The persuasion architecture is the same in both. Write it once, adapt it for the other medium, and test the argument before you pay for production
</KeyTakeaways>

<DefinitionBox term="TSL (Text Sales Letter)">
A long-form sales page that delivers the full direct-response persuasion sequence as scrollable text: hook, story, mechanism, proof, offer, guarantee, close. The web descendant of the printed sales letter. The reader controls the pace, so a TSL is written to sell on its subheads to a skimmer and on its body to a reader. A VSL (video sales letter) delivers the same sequence as a spoken video, where the writer controls the pace.
</DefinitionBox>

## The one difference that explains everything

A VSL is heard. A TSL is read. Everything else follows from that.

When a viewer presses play on a [video sales letter](/blog/what-is-a-vsl), I decide the order they meet the argument in. They can't skip to the price, because the price isn't on screen. If the first ninety seconds earn their attention, they get the story, the mechanism and the proof in the sequence I built, and by the time the price arrives it has been framed. That control is why VSLs took over cold-traffic direct response from 2007 onward.

When a reader lands on a text sales letter, they're in charge. They skim the subheads, scroll to see how long it is, check the price, and decide whether to come back up and read. A TSL has to sell twice: once to the skimmer, through subheads and bolded lines, and once to the reader who stays. That's harder to write well, which is why so many TSLs underperform. It's also why a good one converts readers who would have closed a video after twenty seconds.

So the question is really about your buyer's behaviour at the moment they arrive. And that is set by the traffic source.

## The traffic-source rule

Here's the pattern I've seen across my own funnels and the ones I've reviewed as Copy Chief, and it matches what Stefan Georgi has published from his own testing.

**Native and YouTube traffic: the VSL usually wins.** A reader arriving from a native ad or a YouTube pre-roll has already opted into a story. They're in a lean-back, consume mode, and a VSL that controls the sequence outperforms a page they'd skim.

**Facebook and Instagram traffic: the TSL often matches or beats the VSL.** The reader arrives from a scroll. They're in lean-forward, skim mode, and a twenty-minute video asks for a commitment they haven't made yet. A TSL lets them skim the subheads, find the hook that matches the ad, and read on their own terms. The Abundant TSL I wrote reached 340 percent ROAS on Facebook cold traffic as a long-form, story-driven text page, and I don't think a video would have done better with that audience.

**Email traffic: roughly even.** A subscriber already trusts the sender, so the format matters less than the offer and the argument. Build whichever is cheaper, which is the TSL.

**Retargeting: the TSL.** A visitor who has already seen the video and left rarely watches it again. A text page gives them a different way in.

There are exceptions in every direction, and the right answer for your offer is a split test. But if you have to choose without one, the traffic source is the best predictor I know.

## What each format costs

A TSL costs the copywriting and the page. A senior direct-response writer charges roughly $15,000 to $35,000 for a long-form sales page, and the build is a day or two of a developer's time in most page builders.

A VSL costs the script and then production. Senior VSL scripts run $20,000 to $50,000, and production adds anywhere from $500 for a voiceover and slides to $25,000 for on-camera talent and a professional edit. AI video production has pulled the low end down a long way in 2026; I produce test versions of my own scripts through an AI studio stack before a client pays for a human shoot. I've broken the full ranges down in [how much a VSL costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-vsl-cost).

The number most owners forget is the testing cost. A TSL headline test is a text change. A VSL hook test is a new recording, a new edit and a new render. Over a quarter, that difference decides how much you learn about your market.

## Compliance: the TSL has a real edge

If you sell supplements, financial products or anything else Meta and Google review hard, the TSL is easier to live with. Every claim is on the page where a reviewer and your own compliance check can read it. When a line gets flagged, you change the text and resubmit. A flagged VSL line means new audio, a new edit and a new upload, and the ad stays down while that happens.

A VSL can still be compliant. Mine are written with compliance built in from the first line, and the [compliance checker](https://github.com/robpalmer99/claude-code-copywriting-skills) in my Claude Code skills runs over every script before it ships. The difference is that a mistake costs more in video, so the writing has to be right first time.

## The hybrid page: what most scaled offers actually run

Walk through the top offers in supplements and info products and you'll find the same page shape: a video at the top, a full text sales letter below it, often revealed after a delay so the first few minutes of the video get a fair hearing.

The hybrid serves both buyers. The viewer who prefers to watch, watches. The reader who arrived from Facebook and wants to skim, scrolls. The retargeted visitor who already saw the video finds a different way in. And the page carries the full argument in text for anyone whose sound is off.

The trap is building the two halves separately, with different hooks and different promises. The ad, the video and the text should tell one story. I write the VSL and the TSL from the same angle map so they do, and when I'm engaged as a [VSL copywriter](/services/vsl-copywriter) the TSL adaptation is part of the same project.

## If you can only build one first

Here's the sequence I'd follow for a new offer with a limited budget.

**Match your main traffic source.** Facebook-first offer: write the TSL. Native or YouTube-first: write the VSL.

**Validate the argument in text where you can.** If the economics allow, a TSL is the cheaper place to find out whether the hook, the mechanism and the offer work. Headline and lead tests are nearly free. Once the argument is proven, scripting it as a VSL is low risk.

**Adapt, don't restart.** A winning TSL gives you a validated persuasion architecture to script. A winning VSL gives you a script to rewrite for the eye. Either way the second format costs a fraction of the first.

**Build the hybrid when both exist.** Video on top, text below, one story.

**Then test the thing that matters.** Length, hook and offer. Not format. Format was decided by the traffic.
