A direct-response specialist who writes [video sales letters](/blog/what-is-a-vsl): spoken scripts, usually 10 to 45 minutes long, engineered to take a cold viewer from the first second to the order button. ## What a VSL copywriter has to do that a page writer doesn't

A sales page is read. The reader controls the pace, skims the subheads, jumps to the price, and comes back if the proof earns it. A VSL is heard. The writer controls the pace, and the viewer either stays for the next thirty seconds or closes the tab. That changes the job in five ways, and the writer you hire needs all five.

**Writing for the ear.** Sentences that read well on a page often fail when spoken. A VSL copywriter writes in spoken rhythm, says every number with its unit, and never leaves a line the voice artist can't perform.

**Planning the retention curve.** The script has to survive minute one, minute three, minute six and the price reveal. A good VSL writer knows where viewers leave and builds the pattern interrupt before that point, not after.

**Protecting the reveal.** Nothing about buying, price or guarantees can appear before the product is announced, because the moment a viewer senses a pitch, every proof beat after it reads as setup. Page writers break this rule constantly because a reader can scroll past it. A viewer can't.

**Writing variations from the start.** Three or four hooks and two closes, written with the script, so the first split test is ready on launch day. Bolting variations on later costs a second project fee.

**Delivering a production-ready script.** Slide-by-slide notation, timing cues, on-screen text. Your editor should never have to guess what the writer meant.

If a copywriter's portfolio is sales pages and emails, with one VSL, they may still be excellent. They are not a VSL specialist, and on a script that decides whether your offer scales, the difference shows up in the retention curve.

## The five questions that expose a VSL copywriter

Ask these on the first call. Each one is hard to fake.

**"Which VSLs did you write, and what did each one do on cold traffic?"** You want conversion rates, spend levels and how long the script held as the control. Vague revenue claims with no named offers are a yellow flag.

**"How do you decide length?"** The answer should involve the price point, the traffic temperature and the awareness level of the audience. A writer who always writes 30 minutes, or always writes short, is applying a template. If the writer also asks whether you need the [text version](/blog/vsl-vs-tsl) for Facebook traffic, that is a good sign.

**"How many hooks and closes come with the script?"** The number should be more than one, and they should be part of the project, not an add-on.

**"Show me a production handoff."** Ask to see what the editor received on a past project. If it's a Google Doc of prose, your editor will be making creative decisions the writer should have made.

**"What would you change about my current VSL?"** Send the script and the retention data before the call. A real specialist comes back with the minute mark where viewers leave and a hypothesis about why. A generalist comes back with compliments.

## Red flags that mean you are buying a draft, not a VSL

**They want to start writing this week.** The research is where the conversion rate lives. A writer who skips it is relying on a template, and templates are what every competitor in your market is already running.

**They've never written for your traffic source.** A VSL for Meta cold traffic, with its compliance rules and three-second hook window, is a different animal from a VSL for a warm email list. Ask which they've done.

**They can't talk about retention.** If the words "hold rate", "retention curve" or "where viewers leave" never come up, the writer thinks of a VSL as a long sales page read aloud.

**The price was quoted before they saw the offer.** Scope determines research, research determines results. A number before scoping is a number for a template.

**They talk about themselves more than your market.** The best VSL writers are obsessively curious about your buyer, because that is where the hook comes from.
