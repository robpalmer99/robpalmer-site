Hiring a VSL copywriter is a different job from hiring a copywriter. A sales page writer can show you a page and you can read it. A VSL writer shows you a script, and the thing that made it work, the pacing that held a cold viewer to minute twelve, is invisible on the page. So most buyers hire on reputation, or on price, and find out six weeks later which one they got.

I've been on both sides of this. I've written the [video sales letters](/services/vsl-copywriter) behind a $40 million supplement funnel, the number one offer on ClickBank and a $27 million financial promotion, and I've been hired by Stefan Georgi as Copy Chief to review other professional copywriters' scripts. This guide is what I'd tell a friend who was about to spend real money on a VSL: what to send, what to ask, what to walk away from, and how long it should take.

## Decide what you're hiring for first

Three situations send people looking for a VSL copywriter, and the right hire differs for each.

**You have a VSL and it's underperforming.** You want a control-beater, not a fresh start. The writer should read your retention curve, find where the viewers leave, and rewrite from that point and whatever feeds it. A control-beat is the fastest win in the format and the easiest to judge, because the old script sets the bar.

**You have an offer and no VSL.** You want a writer who starts with research, builds a mechanism, and chooses the length from the price point and the traffic temperature rather than from habit. Check first that a VSL is the right format for your traffic. On Facebook and Instagram a [text sales letter often wins](/blog/vsl-vs-tsl); on native and YouTube the VSL usually does.

**You need volume.** New hooks every week, VSL variations for different audiences, a mini-VSL for ads. That's a creative-team job, and a single specialist who writes one script a month will frustrate you. I cover the trade-off in [is a copywriting agency worth it](/blog/is-a-copywriting-agency-worth-it).

Knowing which of the three you are also tells you what to send.

## What to send before the first call

A good VSL writer wants to see the numbers before they quote, because the numbers are the brief. Send these, and you'll get a diagnosis on the first call instead of a discovery questionnaire.

- **The current script or page**, with the video link if it's live.
- **The retention curve** from your video host, or at least the points where viewers drop. If you don't have it, say so; the writer will know what that means about your tracking.
- **Traffic source and daily spend.** Native, YouTube, Meta, email. The source changes the opening the script needs.
- **Front-end conversion rate, average order value and back-end numbers.** The writer needs to know what a visitor is worth to tell you whether a rewrite pays.
- **Your best-performing ads.** The hook that's already winning in the account is the strongest clue to the hook the VSL should open on.
- **Compliance constraints.** Health claims, financial disclosures, platform history. In supplements and finance this shapes the script from the first line.

If a writer quotes without asking for any of this, they're quoting for a draft.

## The seven questions that expose a specialist

Ask every candidate the same seven. You're listening for specifics, and for whether they volunteer the number before you ask for it.

1. **Which VSLs did you write, and what did each one do on cold traffic?** The answer should be a list with conversion rates, not a portfolio link. A writer who has shipped VSLs knows their numbers the way a pitcher knows their ERA.
2. **How long did your best one hold as the control?** Launch spikes are easy. A script that still converts a year later was built on the market, not on novelty.
3. **How do you decide VSL length?** The right answer involves the price point, the traffic temperature and the awareness level of the audience. The wrong answer is a number.
4. **How many hooks and closes come with the script?** A specialist writes three or four hooks and two closes from the start, because the hook is the highest-leverage test in the funnel and nobody gets it right first time.
5. **What does your production handoff include?** Slide-by-slide notation, timing cues, on-screen text. If the editor has to interpret the script, the VSL that gets recorded isn't the one that was written.
6. **What would you change about my current script?** You sent it before the call. A specialist has already watched it and has a view. A generalist asks what you think the problem is.
7. **What happens if the first version doesn't beat the control?** You want a writer who expects to iterate and has a plan for reading the data, not one who treats delivery as the end of the engagement.

Most writers will do well on three or four of these. The ones you want do well on all seven, and usually answer the sixth before you ask it.

## Red flags that mean you're buying a draft

I've reviewed enough scripts from other writers to know the patterns that predict a VSL that reads well and sells badly.

- **No VSLs in the portfolio.** Sales pages, emails and ads are evidence of copywriting ability. They're not evidence of the pacing and retention instincts a VSL needs. The formats are related, not interchangeable.
- **Results stated as views, watch time or engagement.** VSLs are measured on conversion and earnings per click. A writer who leads with views is either hiding the conversion number or doesn't have it.
- **A turnaround of a few days.** Research, competitor teardown and mechanism development take two to three weeks when done properly. A fast script is a script with the research skipped, and the research is where the conversion rate lives.
- **One hook, one close.** That's a draft with no testing plan.
- **No mention of production.** A VSL script that ships without notation is half a deliverable.
- **A fee under about $3,000.** I've laid out the market bands in [how much does a VSL cost](/blog/how-much-does-a-vsl-cost). Below that line you're paying for words, not for the architecture.
- **A quote before they've seen your script.** The script and the numbers are the brief. Pricing without them is guessing, and the guess is rarely in your favour.

None of these makes someone a bad writer. They make them the wrong hire for a VSL that has to pay for its own traffic.

## A realistic timeline

Buyers often come to me having already lost a month to a hire that went nowhere, so here's what the calendar should look like when it goes right.

- **Week 1 to 2: shortlist and calls.** Send the materials above to three or four writers. Hold the calls. Ask the seven questions.
- **Days after the calls: proposals.** A specialist sends a fixed quote within a few days. Longer than a week and you're not a priority.
- **Week 3 to 4: research and architecture.** Audience research, competitor teardown, mechanism, awareness-stage entry point, the sequence from hook to close. You should see this work as it's built, not as a single drop at the end.
- **Week 5 to 6: script, hooks, closes, revision.** The writing is the shortest phase. The revision round should be about strategy and clarity, not line edits.
- **Week 7 to 8: production and launch.** Recording, editing, upload, first traffic. The writer should be available to read the first results.

Six to eight weeks from first search to live VSL, if you move promptly on decisions. Rush timelines exist, but the research phase is the one thing I never compress, because every VSL I've seen fail fast was a VSL where the research was skipped.

## Where to look

Searching for a VSL copywriter near you is the wrong filter. The work happens over calls and shared documents, and the writer with the strongest results in your vertical is almost never in your city. The useful filters are vertical experience, cold-traffic results and current availability.

I keep a ranked list of the [best VSL copywriters](/blog/best-vsl-copywriters) with those three things checked for each writer, including who still takes client work, since most of the famous names in the format now coach or run their own offers. Copy Accelerator and the larger direct-response communities are the other place senior VSL writers are visible, and a referral from an operator who has paid one of them is worth more than any portfolio.

## How I run it

I write VSLs the way this guide describes because this guide is my process written down. Research first, from the buyer's own words. Mechanism and awareness-stage entry point before a line of script. Three or four hooks and two closes from day one. Slide-by-slide notation so the editor never interprets. And a conversation about the first results, because the script that ships is the start of the test, not the end of it.
