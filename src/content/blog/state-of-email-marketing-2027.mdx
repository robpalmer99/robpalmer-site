## Where Does Email Marketing Stand Going Into 2027?

Email is still the most profitable channel most direct-response businesses have, and it's harder to see what it's doing than it has ever been.

Sinch Mailgun surveyed more than 1,200 email senders for its 2026 Email Impact Report. As [Mailjet summarized it](https://mailjet.com/blog/marketing/email-in-2026-strong-roi-rising-ai-adoption-and-a-measurement-problem-nobodys-fixing/), "60% of senders who measure ROI for promotional emails say it is greater than $10 for every $1 spent." In the same survey, only "46% of senders say they can measure the ROI of promotional emails." So the channel works, and more than half the people using it can't prove it.

I've been writing direct-response email since the inbox was plain text, as part of 40 years in this business and $523M+ in tracked results across the record. This report covers what changed in the last year, what the published data says, and what I see in my own campaigns. Where a number is mine, I'll say so.

## What Changed Since the 2026 Edition?

I published the first edition in February 2026. Here's what moved.

**Gmail's AI reached everyone.** In January 2026 Google [announced](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gmail/gmail-is-entering-the-gemini-era/) that "AI Overview conversation summaries are rolling out today for everyone at no cost," and introduced an AI Inbox that "filters out the clutter so you can focus on what's most important." In October 2026 the AI Inbox [reached paid Workspace users](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/gmails-ai-inbox-is-rolling-out-to-paid-Workspace-users.html), with a line every B2B sender should note: "There is no admin control for this feature."

**Gmail's sender rules now bite.** Google's [sender guidelines FAQ](https://support.google.com/a/answer/14229414) says: "Starting November 2025, Gmail is ramping up its enforcement on non-compliant traffic." Mail that fails the requirements gets temporary and permanent rejections. A year on, that's the normal state of things.

**Unsubscribing got easier.** Gmail's [Manage subscriptions](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gmail/new-manage-subscriptions-unsubscribe/) view lists a user's senders, the most frequent first, with one-click unsubscribe beside each. If you mail often and add little, you're now at the top of a list built for leaving.

**Subject lines became a litigation target.** A Washington State ruling in April 2025 set off more than a hundred lawsuits over misleading subject lines. I cover it below.

**The federal subscription rule stalled.** The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was struck down in court in July 2025, and in March 2026 the agency [asked for public comment](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-seeks-public-comment-response-advance-notice-proposed-rulemaking-regarding-negative-option) on starting again.

### What I got wrong, and right, last year

I said inbox AI would keep getting more sophisticated and that lazy sending would be punished. That held, and the tools arrived faster than I expected.

I said the copywriting layer would stay human because AI can't manufacture voice. I'd put that differently now. AI drafts a good share of my email copy inside a written method, as I explained in my [State of AI Copywriting](/blog/state-of-ai-copywriting-2027) report. What stays with me is the angle of the sequence and its first emails, which set the voice.

I said revenue per subscriber was the number to watch. Everything that has happened to open tracking since has made that more true.

I described "inbox AI" mostly as a filter that decides where your email lands. That was too narrow. The bigger change is that the inbox now summarizes and ranks what it lets through.

## What Does Inbox AI Do to a Marketing Email?

It reads the email before your subscriber does, and it may show them its own version.

Start with what the providers have said. Gmail summarizes conversations for every user. Its AI Inbox pulls out what it judges important. [Apple Mail](https://support.apple.com/en-hk/104971) "automatically sorts your emails into four categories," Primary, Transactions, Updates and Promotions, and says that "Promotions includes emails from businesses and organizations." Yahoo Mail's [Catch Up](https://www.yahooinc.com/press/yahoo-rolls-out-new-catch-up-feature-to-help-users-tackle-inbox-overload) feature lets users clear unread mail from AI summaries. None of these companies publishes how the ranking works, so everything past this point is my reading and not theirs.

Is it hurting results? The only measurement I've found is from the audience-data company Omeda, [reported by MediaCat](https://mediacat.uk/ai-summaries-are-affecting-email-clicks-according-to-study/amp/). Its report covered 2.03 billion emails sent in the second quarter of 2025. Against the quarter before, open rates rose while unique click-through "fell from 4.35% to 3.93%." Omeda suggested Gmail's summaries could be the cause, and the report is frank that this can't be confirmed. It's one data set, and it's a year old. I'd treat it as a reason to watch your own click rate, not as proof.

Here's how I've changed the way I write for it.

**The first two lines carry the point.** If a summary is built from the email, the email has to state what it's about and why it matters before it does anything else. A long wind-up before the idea gets summarized as nothing.

**One email, one idea.** An email with three offers summarizes as a list. An email with one idea summarizes as that idea.

**The words have to be in the text.** An email that is one large image gives a summary nothing to read.

**The subject line and the opening line agree.** A subject line that teases something the email doesn't deliver looked bad before. Now a machine may put the two side by side.

## Can You Still Trust Open Rates?

No. You can use them as a rough signal within one list over time, and that's all.

Litmus publishes [email client market share](https://www.litmus.com/email-client-market-share) from its tracking data. For October 2026, "calculated from over 1.7 billion opens," Apple accounted for 75.84 percent and Gmail for 17.02 percent. Litmus adds the important caveat itself: opens from people using Apple's Mail Privacy Protection "are not considered reliable opens." Apple's software fetches the email whether or not a person reads it.

The size of the distortion is startling. In its commentary on the UK Data & Marketing Association's 2026 benchmark report, [Validity cites](https://www.validity.com/blog/the-2026-dma-email-benchmark-report-what-the-numbers-really-mean-for-revenue/) data from Zeta Global "showing unique opens of 40 percent against a 'true open' rate, with mailbox provider inflation stripped out, of just 11 percent."

So when a platform tells you 40 percent of your list opened an email, the honest reading is that you don't know. The numbers I report to clients are clicks, orders and revenue per subscriber.

<DefinitionBox term="Revenue Per Subscriber (RPS)">
The revenue attributed to email in a month, divided by the number of active subscribers. It combines deliverability, engagement and conversion in one figure, and it can't be inflated by a privacy feature that opens emails on a reader's behalf. It answers the question an owner cares about: what is each name on this list worth to the business each month?
</DefinitionBox>

These are my own working numbers from the direct-response programs I write for and advise, not a published survey:

- **Top programs:** $3 to $8 per subscriber per month. Clean lists, full automation, strong copy and offers that fit.
- **Average programs:** $0.50 to $1.50. Decent lists run mostly on broadcasts.
- **Weak programs:** under $0.50. Large, tired lists mailed the same promotion.

## What Do the Mailbox Providers Require Now?

Authenticated mail, an easy way out, and a low complaint rate. Miss them and your mail is refused.

**Gmail.** Google's [sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126) apply the bulk rules to anyone sending "more than 5,000 messages per day" to Gmail accounts. "Marketing messages and subscribed messages must support one-click unsubscribe." On complaints, Google says to keep reported spam rates "below 0.10% and avoid ever reaching a spam rate of 0.30% or higher."

**Yahoo.** Its [sender best practices](https://senders.yahooinc.com/best-practices/) are short: "Keep your spam rate below 0.3%" and "Honor unsubscribes within 2 days."

**Outlook.com.** Microsoft applies its rules when "you send 5,000 or more email messages to Microsoft consumer email services," and [its support page](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/outlook/fix-ndr-error-550-5-7-515-in-outlook-com) shows the bounce a non-compliant sender gets: the sending domain "does not meet the required authentication level."

Most businesses aren't fully there. Valimail's [2026 State of DMARC report](https://www.valimail.com/?p=11937) found that "actual enforcement has plateaued at just 42 percent." Many domains have published a DMARC record to tick the box and left it at a setting that doesn't stop anyone forging their address.

I went through this on my own domains in September 2026. I set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC, started at a monitoring-only policy, read the reports for a few weeks to make sure nothing legitimate was failing, and then moved to quarantine. None of it is difficult. In most businesses it's simply a job nobody owns until the bounces start.

One number in those rules deserves a second look. A complaint rate of 0.3 percent is three people in a thousand pressing the spam button. A single badly judged promotion to a cold segment can do that, which is why the oldest advice in email, stop mailing people who never engage, now protects your delivery as well as your pride.

## Where Does Email Revenue Come From?

Mostly from the emails you write once.

Klaviyo's [2026 benchmarks](https://www.klaviyo.com/products/email-marketing/benchmarks), "based on over 183,000 Klaviyo customers," report that campaigns make up 94.7 percent of send volume, yet "flows generate nearly 41% of total email revenue from just 5.3% of sends." Average revenue per recipient from flows is "nearly 18x higher than campaigns." Click rates tell the same story: "1.69% for campaigns and 5.58% for flows."

Omnisend's [benchmark report](https://www.omnisend.com/blog/email-marketing-benchmarks/), covering more than 27,000 brands, found that "automated emails generated $3.41 per email sent in 2025, compared with $0.155 for campaign emails," and that "automated emails converted at 1.49%, while campaign emails converted at 0.08%."

The reason is timing. A flow email arrives because the subscriber just did something: joined, browsed, abandoned a cart, bought, or went quiet. A campaign arrives because it's Tuesday.

That has an obvious consequence for where the writing budget goes. The sequence I wrote for Chatterkick, a social media management platform, was seven emails triggered by what a trial user did and didn't do, and it lifted trial-to-paid conversion by 22 percent. That's seven emails, written once.

For an e-commerce or supplement business the order I'd write them in is: welcome, abandoned cart, post-purchase, replenishment or pre-renewal, and win-back. I've set out each one in [e-commerce email flows](/blog/ecommerce-email-flows), and the SaaS version in [SaaS onboarding sequences](/blog/saas-email-onboarding-sequences).

Campaigns still earn their place for launches, promotions and the regular contact that keeps a list warm. But if your flows are the templates your email platform shipped with, that's the first thing to fix.

## What Kind of Email Copy Is Working?

Copy that sounds like one person writing to another about one thing.

**One angle through the whole sequence.** For Ben Palmer, a ClickBank Platinum vendor, I wrote an email-driven health funnel with every email built on the same angle. It gets 300 percent ROAS. A sequence where each email tries a different argument teaches the reader nothing. A sequence that comes at one belief from seven directions builds it.

**Story over announcement.** In my campaigns, story-driven sequences have lifted revenue per subscriber by 40 to 60 percent over straight promotional ones. That's my working number. The offers were the same. The reader simply had a reason to open the next one.

**A plain format.** I default to emails that look like a message and not a brochure. I haven't found a recent independent test I'd rest that on, so take it as my practice. The summary problem above is a second reason for it.

**Short subject lines that tell the truth.** I keep subject lines to about 40 characters so they survive a phone screen, and I've written up the method in [how to write email subject lines](/blog/how-to-write-email-subject-lines). The truth part used to be good manners. As the next section shows, it's now also a legal matter.

**A real person's name on it.** Mail from a named person, written in that person's voice, gets treated as correspondence by the reader. That's the whole reason the old direct-mail letter was signed.

## What Is Legally Riskier Than It Was a Year Ago?

Subject lines, and anything to do with recurring billing. I'm a copywriter and this isn't legal advice.

**Misleading subject lines.** In April 2025 the Washington Supreme Court ruled, in a case against Old Navy, that the state's Commercial Electronic Mail Act prohibits any false or misleading information in the subject line of a commercial email. The law firm [Ballard Spahr reported](https://www.ballardspahr.com/insights/alerts-and-articles/2026/04/cema-ingly-endless-litigation-brown-v-old-navy-turns-1-year-old) a year later that "a whopping 105 CEMA lawsuits have been filed since Brown was decided in April 2025," seeking "statutory penalties of $500 per violation." Most of the suits have targeted retailers. According to the firm, many concern subject lines about "limited time" or "expiring" sales that were later extended, and others target "free" gifts that turn out to need a purchase. You don't need to be based in Washington to have Washington subscribers.

**The federal baseline.** The FTC's [CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says "each separate email in violation of the CAN-SPAM Act is subject to penalties of up to $53,088." Its rules are the familiar ones: no deceptive subject lines, a working opt-out, and opt-outs honored within 10 business days.

**Subscriptions and auto-ship.** The click-to-cancel rule is gone for now. As [Gibson Dunn put it](https://gibsondunn.com/ftc-restarts-negative-option-rulemaking-after-eighth-circuit-vacatur-enforcement-under-rosca-continues/), "in July 2025, the Eighth Circuit vacated the Click to Cancel Rule," and the FTC "continues to regulate subscription practices through enforcement" under an older law. If your emails sell a subscribe-and-save offer or a free trial that bills later, the terms belong in the email in plain words.

The fake deadline is the common thread. "Last chance" on an offer that returns next week has always cost trust. In one state it now costs $500 a time. I covered the UK regulator's fines for fake countdown timers in my [State of VSL Marketing](/blog/state-of-vsl-marketing-2027) report, and the direction is the same everywhere.

## Where I'd Start in 2027

In this order.

1. **Make sure the mail arrives.** Authenticate, move DMARC to enforcement, add one-click unsubscribe, and watch the complaint rate.
2. **Rewrite the flows.** They're a small share of your sends and a large share of your revenue.
3. **Change the scoreboard.** Revenue per subscriber, month by month.
4. **Give each sequence one angle.** Then write the emails.
5. **Clean the list.** A smaller list that reads your email earns more than a big one that doesn't.
6. **Audit your subject lines** against what the emails deliver.
