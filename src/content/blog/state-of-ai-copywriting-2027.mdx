## Where Does AI Copywriting Stand Going Into 2027?

AI writes most of the first drafts in marketing now, and it has made copy faster and cheaper without making it better. That gap is the whole story of the year.

The Content Marketing Institute's [2026 B2B research](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/articles/b2b-content-marketing-trends-research) surveyed 861 B2B marketers. Eighty-eight percent use AI content tools. Asked what AI contributes, they said speed (78 percent), efficiency (59 percent) and volume (44 percent). Quality came in at 17 percent. Their two most common challenges, at 54 percent each, were ensuring accuracy and maintaining quality control.

I've been writing direct-response copy for 40 years, with $523M+ in tracked results across the record. I've been experimenting with AI writing tools for at least two years and have kept logs of what they produce for the last year. I use them every working day. This report covers what the data says, what I've seen in my own work, and where I've changed my mind.

For the fundamentals I've written separate guides: [AI copywriting](/blog/ai-copywriting) for the overview, [AI vs human copywriting](/blog/ai-vs-human-copywriting) for the head-to-head, and [AI copywriting tools](/blog/ai-copywriting-tools) for the tool comparison.

## What Changed Since the 2026 Edition?

I published the first edition in February 2026. Six things moved.

**The models turned over again.** As I write, the flagships are Anthropic's [Claude Fable 5.2](https://www.anthropic.com/news), OpenAI's [GPT-6.2 Astra](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol/) and Google's [Gemini 4 Argon](https://gemini.google/us/release-notes/), all released in the closing months of 2026. Each is better at following a long, detailed brief than what it replaced. None of them has made the brief unnecessary.

**Skills became a shared standard.** Anthropic describes [agent skills](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills) as "organized folders of instructions, scripts, and resources that agents can discover and load dynamically." In October 2026 it [handed the standard to a foundation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/donating-agent-skills-to-the-agentic-ai-foundation) and reported that "more than 70 agent platforms, including ChatGPT, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and VS Code" had adopted it. A method you write down once now runs in almost any tool.

**The US regulator eased off the tools.** In December 2025 the FTC [reopened and set aside its order against Rytr](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-reopens-sets-aside-rytr-final-order-response-trump-administrations-ai-action-plan), an AI writing service, finding that "the order unduly burdens innovation in the nascent AI industry." The rules on what your copy claims didn't ease at all, which I'll come back to.

**Europe's transparency rules took effect.** The AI Act's Article 50 has applied since August 2026. For text, its reach is narrower than most summaries suggest.

**The job data caught up.** Government projections and large studies of freelance platforms now show what writers have been saying for two years.

**I started letting AI write first versions.** In the 2026 edition I wrote that top copywriters use AI for research and ideas, "not as a drafting replacement." That's no longer how I work, and I'd rather say so than quietly edit it.

### What I got wrong, and right, last year

I underrated drafting. With a proper method wrapped around it, a model now produces a usable first version of most formats. The method is the hard part, and I'll describe mine below.

I said specialized AI copywriting platforms were mostly wrappers around the same models. I'd stand by that, and the skills standard has made it more true: the method no longer needs a platform.

I said the middle of the market would be squeezed from both sides. The job data below bears that out.

I said the gap on strategic work would narrow slowly. It has narrowed on fluency and structure. On choosing the angle, building the mechanism and knowing which claim a market will believe, I don't see it closing.

## Does AI-Written Copy Convert as Well as Human Copy?

Nobody has published a clean answer, and you should be wary of anyone who quotes you one.

Two studies are worth knowing. The first, [published in the Journal of Brand Strategy](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aza/jbs000/y2025v14i1p30-42.html) in 2025, tested human and AI copy in Meta ads and found that "human-generated copies outperformed AI in 9 out of 12 cases for cost-effectiveness and in 5 out of 12 cases for CTR effectiveness." So the humans won clearly on cost per result and lost narrowly on clicks. It's a small study, and I've only been able to read the abstract.

The second is a [field experiment by Harang Ju and Sinan Aral](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18238), last revised in October 2026, which "randomly assigned 2,234 participants to human-human and human-AI teams that produced 11,024 ads." The human-AI teams produced 50 percent more ads per worker and wrote higher-quality ad text. Their output was also more alike from one team to the next. When the ads ran on X, better text went with better click-through.

Put those together and you get a fair picture of short copy. A person working with AI writes more, and often better, ad text than a person working alone, and the results converge on a house style unless someone pushes against it.

What I can't find is a published controlled test of long-form sales copy: a sales page, a VSL, an email sequence carrying real ad spend. So here I can only give you my own experience. In my own tests, an unedited AI draft of a sales page loses to a page I've built, and the reason is always upstream of the sentences. The draft picks the obvious angle, borrows a mechanism the market has already heard and proves it with nothing in particular.

The long-form text sales page I wrote for Stonehenge Health's Dynamic Brain produced $2.1M in its first 90 days, as reported by the client. You can read an excerpt in my [portfolio](/portfolio). That page rests on research, a named condition and a mechanism. Those are decisions, and they were made before a word of copy existed.

## How Do Readers React to AI Copy?

They've noticed, and they've named it.

Merriam-Webster chose "slop" as its 2025 word of the year, defined, [as PBS reported](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/merriam-websters-word-of-the-year-for-2025-is-ais-slop), as "digital content of low quality that is produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence."

Gartner has put numbers on the mood. In a [survey of 1,539 US consumers](https://gcom.pdo.aws.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-16-gartner-marketing-survey-finds-50-percent-of-consumers-prefer-brands-that-avoid-using-genai-in-consumer-facing-content0), half said they "would prefer to give their business to brands that do not use GenAI" in consumer-facing content. In a smaller [survey of 307](https://gcom.pdo.aws.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-06-09-gartner-survey-finds-49-of-u-s-consumers-say-genai-has-made-content-quality-worse), 49 percent agreed "that GenAI has made the quality of content available worse." Those are stated preferences. People don't check how a page was written before they buy. But they do stop reading copy that feels generated, and that's the practical risk.

Meanwhile the industry hasn't decided what to tell them. In the CMI survey, 52 percent of marketers said their organization has no policy on disclosing AI use.

<DefinitionBox term="AI Slop">
Low-quality content produced in quantity with AI. In marketing it describes copy that is fluent and grammatically clean but says nothing specific: no named result, no real customer, no claim that belongs to this product and no other. Slop fails because a reader can tell within a few lines that nobody who knows the subject made a decision about what to say.

### The tells I edit out

Every draft that leaves my desk gets a sweep for the patterns that give a machine away. This is the list I work from, built up from corrections on real client jobs.

- **The "it's not this, it's that" construction**, including the version split across two sentences. Once you see it you'll find it in every AI draft.
- **Runs of short fragments.** One punchy fragment lands. Three in a row, repeated down the page, reads as generated.
- **Dashes by default.** I allow one only where nothing else works.
- **Lists that always come in threes**, and paragraphs that all keep the same rhythm.
- **Abstract verbs.** A word that's fine when it's literal becomes a tell the moment it's used as a metaphor for business progress.
- **The same idea twice.** Models repeat a point two hundred lines apart in different words, where a word-frequency check won't catch it.
- **Emoji on every line** of an ad, and stiff, uncontracted sentences in copy that's meant to sound like a person talking.

Fixing these by search-and-replace makes things worse. A mechanical pass to add contractions, for one, breaks English in ways that read as careless. It has to be done by reading.

## Does Google Penalize AI Content?

Google penalizes pages made in bulk that give the reader nothing, and it says plainly that the tool isn't the issue.

Its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) define scaled content abuse as "when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users," and list as an example "using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users." Its [guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) adds a warning every marketer should pin up: generative models "don't retrieve facts, but predict a likely sequence of words based on their training data."

The search results agree with the policy. Ahrefs [studied top-ranking pages](https://ahrefs.com/blog/google-doesnt-punish-ai-content/) in June 2026 and found that 5.3 percent of pages in positions one to three were fully AI-generated, while pages with under half AI content made up 82.2 percent. AI detection is imperfect, as Ahrefs says itself, but the shape is clear. Pure AI pages can rank. Mostly they don't.

I learned the practical version of this on my own site. In November 2026 I checked which of my blog posts Google had actually indexed and found it had declined about fifty of them. None was short; they ran from 3,300 to 7,000 words. What the rejected posts shared was that they made claims without a named case behind them and linked to no outside source. I rewrote every one with real results and checked references, and retired a dozen that didn't deserve to exist. Word count had nothing to do with it. Google was asking for evidence.

There's a second change that matters more to anyone who lives on search traffic. Pew Research [tracked 900 US adults](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) and found that people who saw an AI summary clicked a traditional result "in 8% of all visits," against 15 percent for those who didn't. A page that answers a simple question now feeds the summary and gets fewer visits. The pages that still earn a click are the ones a buyer needs to read in full. ## What Has AI Done to Copywriting Jobs and Rates?

It cut the demand for writing as a standalone task and raised the value of everything around it.

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics [projects](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/media-and-communication/writers-and-authors.htm) little or no change in employment for writers and authors between 2025 and 2035, about 500 fewer jobs on a base of 140,300, and says that "increasing use of artificial intelligence (AI) for writing is projected to dampen demand for these workers."

Freelance platforms moved faster. Researchers at the [Oxford Internet Institute](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/the-winners-and-losers-of-generative-ai-in-the-freelance-job-market/) analyzed over three million job postings and reported that skills that can be partly substituted, "such as writing and translating, have seen demand drop by 20 to 50 per cent."

Job ads show where the demand went. Semrush [compared content marketing listings](https://www.semrush.com/blog/content-marketing-job-market-study/) with its 2023 data and found the requirement for "writing" down 28 percent in execution-level roles while "content creation" rose 209 percent. Indeed's Hiring Lab [found](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/?p=20272) that mentions of AI in US marketing job postings grew "from 8.4% at the start of the year to 14.9% by the end of December" 2025.

On price, the split I described last year has widened. These are my working numbers and not a published benchmark: routine content that cost $300 to $500 a piece in 2023 can be produced for $50 to $100 with AI and a light edit. Senior direct-response rates have held, and I track the current ranges in [copywriting rates](/blog/copywriting-rates).

The writers doing well have stopped selling words. They sell the decisions: which angle, which mechanism, which twenty ads to test this week. That's the shift from copywriter to creative strategist, and I've set out the difference in [creative strategist vs copywriter](/blog/creative-strategist-vs-copywriter).

## Why Does the Method Now Matter More Than the Model?

Because everyone has the same models, and a model only knows your method if you've written it down.

A skill is the written-down version. It's a folder holding the instructions for one job, the reference material that job needs and any scripts that check the result. When the task comes up, the agent loads the folder and follows it. Since the format became an open standard, a skill written for one tool works in most of the others.

Here's what that looks like in my own work.

**The method is in files.** My process for a sales page, a VSL, an ad or an email sequence lives in skills: the research steps, the structure, the order of the argument, the review. I've released [eight of them free](/blog/claude-code-copywriting-skills), and the [public repository](https://github.com/robpalmer99/claude-code-copywriting-skills) had 60 stars and 11 forks when I checked for this report.

**The corrections are in a file too.** Every time a client or a test result corrects something, the rule goes into a running list that loads before any draft is written. It holds about 90 rules now. The list of tells above comes from it. This is what stops the same mistake arriving in the next draft.

**A second pass argues with the first.** A draft goes through a linter for the mechanical tells and then a separate review whose only job is to find what's weak or missing. Then it comes to me.

**I do three things by hand.** I choose the angle and write the one sentence the piece has to make the reader believe. I rewrite the lead, because AI first drafts are weakest at the top. And I check every figure against its source.

I call the whole arrangement [Triple Brain Marketing](/blog/triple-brain-marketing): the experience, the AI running my skills, and a library of proven copy and frameworks for it to draw on. ## What Does AI Still Get Wrong?

Four things, consistently enough that I plan around them.

**It invents.** Google's warning about predicted words is the polite version. I once had an AI research agent hand me a complete, confident dossier on a prospect that turned out to be fabricated. Nothing a model reports as fact goes into my copy until I've opened the source and found the sentence.

**It writes weak leads.** The opening of an AI draft is the most generic part, because the obvious opening is the likeliest one. The lead decides whether anyone reads the rest, so that's where the hand-rewriting goes.

**It can't cut.** Ask a model to shorten a script and it rewords. In my experience wording alone gets you about 6 percent. Past that someone has to decide which section goes, and that's a judgment about the argument.

**It drifts toward the average.** The Ju and Aral experiment measured it: human-AI teams produced output that was more alike. In a market where every competitor is prompting the same models about the same product category, the average is exactly where you can't afford to be.

## What Do the Rules Say About AI-Written Copy?

Less than you'd expect about the writing, and exactly as much as ever about the claims.

In the United States the FTC's rule on reviews and testimonials covers fake reviews written by AI, and the agency has started enforcing it. I covered the first cases in my [State of VSL Marketing](/blog/state-of-vsl-marketing-2027) report. Setting aside the Rytr order tells you the regulator is no longer going after writing tools as such. It doesn't change your responsibility for what the tool writes.

In the EU, [Article 50 of the AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/) requires disclosure of AI-generated text "published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest," with an exception where the content has been through human review and a person "holds editorial responsibility." Ordinary sales copy isn't obviously in scope. I'm a copywriter and this isn't legal advice, so check your own position.

The ad platforms label AI-made images and video. I haven't found a Meta or Google rule that requires a label on AI-written ad text.

So the constraint is the old one. In health and finance especially, a model will write a claim that sounds reasonable and can't be supported, or describe a mechanism in scientific language that no study backs. Every claim needs a source you can produce, and that review is human work.

## What Should a Business Owner Do in 2027?

Match the method to what the copy is worth.

1. **For copy in front of paid traffic**, hire an experienced copywriter who works with AI, and ask to see the method. If the answer is a prompt, keep looking.
2. **For routine content**, use AI with a named person responsible for accuracy. Google's test and your reader's test are the same: is there anything here a model couldn't have written?
3. **For testing**, use AI hard. Hooks, headlines and subject lines are where volume pays.
4. **For a team that's already producing more and converting no better**, fix the method before you change the tool. Most teams I look at are running one-line prompts into a very capable model.
5. **Write the rules down.** Every correction your best person makes should become a line the next draft has to obey.
